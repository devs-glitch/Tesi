\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Motivation}

In September 2015 the two Advanced LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave
Observatory) detectors made the first direct detection of a gravitational wave,
GW150914, which was produced by the merger of two black holes~\cite{abbott2016gw150914}.
Two years later, LIGO and Virgo jointly observed GW170817, the first signal from a
binary neutron star merger~\cite{abbott2017gw170817}. Its electromagnetic
counterpart, from a short gamma-ray burst to a kilonova, was then followed by
telescopes around the world, an event that marked the beginning of multi-messenger
astronomy~\cite{abbott2017multimessenger}.

Since then, detections have gone from exceptional events to routine ones, leading
to a continuous stream of data in the field of gravitational-wave astronomy,
data that must be analyzed, often in real time. In this setting, the main obstacle is not the
faintness of the astrophysical signals but the noise. The detectors are sensitive
enough to record a constant stream of short, non-Gaussian transients of
instrumental and environmental origin, known as \emph{glitches}. What makes
glitches both intresting and detrimental to astronomical analysis is that they can mimic
genuine signals or overlap with them, which raises the false-alarm rate and reduces
the effective sensitivity of searches.

Gravity Spy, the source of the data used in this work, is an Open Source project
specifically created created to address this problem.
It combines citizen-science classification with machine learning, aiming to
sort glitches into morphological families based on their time--frequency
spectrograms~\cite{zevin2017}.
Knowing which family a glitch belongs to is the first step towards tracing it back
to its physical cause and, where possible, addressing the source of the problem
and ultimately removing that cause at the instrument.

More broadly, machine learning has become indispensable to perform data-quality work, but
it comes at a cost. The demand for computing power is indeed growing faster than the
resources available, public alerts must be issued within minutes of a detection,
and data volumes are expected to grow further with future observing runs and next-generation
detectors. Making models lighter and cheaper to run is therefore a natural goal.
Two approaches are particularly relevant: quantization, which compresses an
existing network, and spiking neural networks (SNNs), which are designed from the outset
for energy-efficient, and event-driven computation.

Running a neural network outside a data center generally means making it smaller,
and the standard tool for this is indeed quantization. Quantization is a practice implemented
to represent a network's weights and activations with fewer bits, trading numerical precision
for savings in memory and energy. A quantized model is routinely validated by checking that its accuracy
has not dropped, and for many applications this is a sufficient test.

It is not sufficient, however, when the model is also expected to explain its
decisions. Rabbi et al.~\cite{rabbi2026} evaluated five convolutional architectures
at INT8 and INT4, comparing the attribution maps of the quantized models with those
of their full-precision counterparts. Their core founding was that explanation similarity
degrades measurably between INT8 and INT4, even where accuracy differences are
negligible. They also discovered that when quantization does cause a misclassification,
the quantized model is generally attending to \emph{different} regions of the input,
rather than weighing the same evidence differently.

In other words, accuracy and interpretability do not degrade together.

This finding, however, is limited to conventional artificial neural networks (ANNs), and
spiking neural networks (SNNs), the architecture most closely associated with
low-power deployment~\cite{roy2019towards}, differ from ANNs in ways that make the
question both harder and more interesting.

\section{Research question}

The question this thesis addresses is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does a quantized spiking neural network under edge-realistic constraints
base its decisions on the same evidence as its full-precision counterpart, and
at what energy cost?}
\end{quote}

Spiking networks possess three properties that are absent in convolutional networks,
and each of them is crucial for framing the research question.

\paragraph{A second quantization target.} A spiking neuron carries a membrane
potential which is a state variable read and written at every time step, and whose storage
scales with the number of neurons, rather than the number of parameters. In the
network studied here it occupies $367.5$\,KiB against $1\,152.7$\,KiB of weights
at full precision, and it becomes the larger share of the footprint as soon as
the weights are compressed. It is a quantization target of its
own~\cite{yin2024mint}, and it can and will be quantized independently of the
weights~\cite{smith2026}, whereas a conventional network offers only weights and
activations.

\paragraph{A temporal axis in the explanation.} A spiking explanation is a
\emph{sequence} of maps, one per time step, which effectively builds a temporal axis in the explanations.
Quantization may preserve where a network looks while displacing when it decides,
a change to which spatial metrics are blind by construction.

\paragraph{A native explainer.} The Spike Activation Map (SAM)~\cite{kim2021sam}
reads spike activity directly. It requires neither gradients, nor side-stepping the
approximation error a surrogate gradient injects into any transplanted
Grad-CAM-style method~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}, nor ground-truth labels.

The working hypothesis follows from the first two properties:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Quantizing the membrane potential degrades the temporal structure of the
explanation before quantizing the weights degrades its spatial structure},
\end{quote}

an hypothesis that originates from the principle that the membrane potential
is the variable that integrates evidence over time,
while a weight error is fixed for a whole forward pass. The hypothesis is
falsifiable, and testing it precisely requires quantizing the two targets separately: the
membrane arm should displace the temporal centre of mass of the explanation at a
\emph{higher} bit-width than the one at which the weight arm lowers its spatial
correlation.

\section{Contributions}

Answering the research question requires an instrument, and the metrics used to
compare explanations return numbers between zero and one whatever is fed to them.
Before a quantization result can be read, one must know what those metrics report
when nothing has changed. The thesis is therefore organized in two parts: the
construction and calibration of the instrument, and its application to the
quantization grid.

To address the research question, it is necessary to employ a measurement tool.
However, since the metrics used throughout the work consistently yield values between zero and
one--regardless of the input data--establishing a reference baseline is essential.
Indeed, before interpreting the final analysis results, one must determine the values
these metrics produce in the absence of variations. The thesis is therefore
structured into two main sections: the first dedicated to the design and calibration
of the tool, and the second to its operational application to the quantization grid.

The core contributions are therefore:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A hardware-realizable full-precision baseline.} At a chosen
        The input resolution of 112\,px based on network health,
        measured as dead and saturated neuron
        fractions, and on the resolution of the explanation maps, not on
        accuracy: at 64\,px the last feature map shrinks to $4\times4$, too
        coarse for spatial metrics. The membrane decay was also searched only among
        values realizable with a shift and a subtraction, and the search
        returned a value of $\beta = 0.75$. Over three seeds the reference reaches
        $0.9833 \pm 0.0014$ validation accuracy.

  \item \textbf{A corrected implementation of the Spike Activation Map.}
        The decay parameter $\gamma$ is fixed on the
        temporal axis it actually governs. The neuron contribution score now
        sums over strictly earlier time steps ($t' < t$), which removes a
        constant pedestal that had inflated map correlations.

  \item \textbf{A calibration of the explanation-divergence metrics} at three
        levels: numerical noise, a thirtyfold change in $\gamma$, and
        independently initialized networks of equal accuracy. Spatial metrics are validated
        only between a model and its own parent, so every quantized model is
        compared with the full-precision counterpart model of its own seed.

  \item \textbf{A bit-width characterization of explanation fidelity.} Weight and membrane quantization are applied
        separately and then together, at 8, 4 and 2 bits and three seeds,
        alongside a full-precision fine-tuning control.

  \item \textbf{An analytical energy and memory account} of every operating
        point, with synaptic operations computed from per-layer firing rates,
        in order that accuracy, explanation fidelity, and energy can be read on a
        common bit-width axis.
\end{enumerate}

The central result is a configuration that the accuracy criterion alone cannot discriminate
from its neighbours. Quantizing the membrane potential to 2 bits leaves
accuracy at parity, $0.982$, yet the region its explanations highlight overlaps
the full-precision one by $0.44$, well below the numerical floor of $0.70$; a
4-bit weight model of the same accuracy keeps an overlap of $0.74$. The change
is real but bounded, as for the classes on which SAM is faithful, the
deletion metric shows that the new explanations still point at evidence the
model uses, but not at the evidence the original model pointed and looked at.

The hypothesis stated above is ultimately not supported. Read
against the floor of each class, the two effects it orders appear at the same
bit-width: at 4 bits the membrane arm moves the temporal centre of mass above
its floor, and the weight arm lowers the spatial correlation below its floor in
all four classes, while moving the centre of mass by a similar amount in the
opposite direction.  At 2 bits the membrane modifies \emph{where} the explanation points more than
\emph{when}. The mechanism behind the hypothesis is supported, as the same
quantization makes the inter-spike intervals of the first layer exactly
regular, with a coefficient of variation of zero in all six models with a 2-bit
membrane, over the $3$ to $28\,\%$ of neurons that fire often enough within
eight steps for an interval to be measured. The temporal metric of the
explanation does not register this change.

The configuration with the changed explanation is also dominated on energy and
memory, so in this network there is no trade-off to make. A joint 4-bit model
keeps accuracy at parity and its explanation at the numerical floor, with one
eighth of the memory and about $1/60$ of the estimated energy if the input image
is quantized to the same width as the weights; if the first layer stays in
floating point, the factor is $1/2.8$.

\section{Application domain}

The classification task is the identification of transient noise --- known as
\emph{glitches} --- in the data of the LIGO gravitational-wave detectors, using
the Gravity Spy release~\cite{zevin2017, glanzer2023, glanzer2021}.

The domain was chosen for three reasons. Explanation fidelity particularly has an
\emph{operational} consequence: glitch classification guides detector
characterization, where a commissioner can act physically on the instrument: a
compressed model reaching the same label from different evidence would preserve
the label but would be corrupting the diagnostic cue.

The classes have physically
interpretable morphology, so a domain expert can judge whether an attention map
falls where the glitch actually is, which is not necessarily the case of a generic image
benchmark. Moreover, the data is public and large, with every trigger labelled by the
Gravity Spy classifier. The labels are machine labels (not a verified
ground truth), and in Section~\ref{sec:gravityspy} it is reported how far the scores 
released with them support them.
