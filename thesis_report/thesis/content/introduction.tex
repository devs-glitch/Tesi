\section{Motivation}

In September 2015 the two Advanced LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave
Observatory) detectors made the first direct detection of a gravitational wave,
GW150914, produced by the merger of two black holes. % TODO: cite discovery paper
Two years later, LIGO and Virgo jointly observed GW170817, the first signal from a
binary neutron star merger. Its electromagnetic counterpart, from a short
gamma-ray burst to a kilonova, was then followed by telescopes around the world,
marking the beginning of multi-messenger astronomy. % TODO: cite GW170817 papers

Since then, detections have gone from exceptional events to routine ones, and
gravitational-wave astronomy now produces a continuous stream of data that must be
analyzed, often in real time. In this setting, the main obstacle is not the
faintness of the astrophysical signals but the noise. The detectors are sensitive
enough to record a constant stream of short, non-Gaussian transients of
instrumental and environmental origin, known as \emph{glitches}. Glitches can mimic
genuine signals or overlap with them, which raises the false-alarm rate and reduces
the effective sensitivity of searches.

Gravity Spy, the source of the data used in this work, was created to address this
problem. By combining citizen-science classification with machine learning, it
sorts glitches into morphological families based on their time--frequency
spectrograms. % TODO: cite Zevin et al. 2017
Knowing which family a glitch belongs to is the first step towards tracing it back
to its physical cause and, where possible, removing that cause at the instrument.

More broadly, machine learning has become indispensable to data-quality work, but
it comes at a cost. The demand for computing power is growing faster than the
resources available, public alerts must be issued within minutes of a detection,
and data volumes will grow further with future observing runs and next-generation
detectors. Making models lighter and cheaper to run is therefore a natural goal.
Two approaches are particularly relevant: quantization, which compresses an
existing network, and spiking neural networks, which are designed from the outset
for energy-efficient, event-driven computation.

Running a neural network outside a data center generally means making it smaller,
and the standard tool for this is indeed quantization. Quantization represents weights
and activations with fewer bits, trading numerical precision for savings in memory
and energy. A quantized model is routinely validated by checking that its accuracy
has not dropped, and for many applications this is a sufficient test.

It is not sufficient, however, when the model is also expected to explain its
decisions. Rabbi et al.~\cite{rabbi2026} evaluated five convolutional architectures
at INT8 and INT4, comparing the attribution maps of the quantized models with those
of their full-precision counterparts. They found that explanation similarity
degrades measurably between INT8 and INT4 even where accuracy differences are
negligible. They also found that when quantization does cause a misclassification,
the quantized model is generally attending to \emph{different} regions of the input,
rather than weighing the same evidence differently.

In other words, accuracy and interpretability do not degrade together.

This finding, however, is limited to conventional artificial neural networks (ANNs).
Spiking neural networks (SNNs), the architecture most closely associated with
low-power deployment~\cite{roy2019towards}, differ from ANNs in ways that make the
question both harder and more interesting.

\section{Research question}

The question this thesis addresses is:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Does a quantized spiking neural network under edge-realistic constraints
base its decisions on the same evidence as its full-precision counterpart, and
at what energy cost?}
\end{quote}

Three properties of spiking networks have no counterpart in the convolutional
setting, and each one bears on the question.

\paragraph{A second quantization target.} A spiking neuron carries a membrane
potential: a state variable read and written at every time step, and a dominant
memory cost at deployment. It is quantized independently of the
weights~\cite{yin2024mint, smith2026}, whereas a conventional network offers only
weights and activations. The two targets can therefore be separated, which turns
a single observation into a causal ablation.

\paragraph{A temporal axis in the explanation.} A spiking explanation is a
\emph{sequence} of maps, one per time step. Quantization may preserve where a
network looks while displacing when it decides, a change to which spatial
metrics are blind by construction.

\paragraph{A native explainer.} The Spike Activation Map~\cite{kim2021sam}
reads spike activity directly. It requires neither gradients, side-stepping the
approximation error a surrogate gradient injects into any transplanted
Grad-CAM-style method~\cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}, nor ground-truth labels.

The working hypothesis follows from the first two properties:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Quantizing the membrane potential degrades the temporal structure of the
explanation before quantizing the weights degrades its spatial structure},
\end{quote}

since the membrane potential is the variable that integrates evidence over time,
while a weight error is fixed for a whole forward pass. The hypothesis is
falsifiable, and testing it requires quantizing the two targets separately: the
membrane arm should displace the temporal centre of mass of the explanation at a
\emph{higher} bit-width than the one at which the weight arm lowers its spatial
correlation. If the order is reversed, the hypothesis is refuted, which is itself
a reportable result.

\section{Contributions}

Answering the research question requires an instrument, and the metrics used to
compare explanations return numbers between zero and one whatever is fed to them.
Before a quantization result can be read, one must know what those metrics report
when nothing has changed. The thesis is therefore organized in two parts: the
construction and calibration of the instrument, and its application to the
quantization grid.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A hardware-realizable full-precision baseline}, with every
        design choice settled on evidence and recorded in a frozen manifest: the
        input resolution chosen on network health rather than accuracy, the
        membrane decay restricted to shift-implementable values inside the
        hyperparameter search rather than rounded afterwards, and a three-seed
        reference at $0.9833 \pm 0.0014$ validation accuracy.

  \item \textbf{A corrected implementation of the Spike Activation Map}, with
        its decay parameter fixed on the temporal axis it actually governs, and
        its faithfulness tested quantitatively against a random baseline rather
        than judged by visual plausibility.

  \item \textbf{A calibration of the explanation-divergence metrics} at three
        levels: numerical noise, a thirtyfold change in the explainer, and
        initialization variance. Neither of the two closest
        works~\cite{rabbi2026, smith2026} establishes any of the three, and the
        result has the widest scope of the thesis: spatial metrics are valid only
        between a model and its own parent.

  \item \textbf{A bit-width characterization of explanation fidelity for a
        spiking classifier}, with weight and membrane quantization separated and
        then combined over thirty fine-tuning runs, measured with spatial and
        temporal divergence metrics read against the calibrated thresholds,
        tested for faithfulness, reported per class and per seed, and stratified
        by event strength.

  \item \textbf{An analytical energy and memory account} of every operating
        point, so that accuracy, explanation fidelity and energy can be read on
        a common bit-width axis.
\end{enumerate}

The central result is a configuration that the accuracy criterion cannot tell
apart from its neighbours. Quantizing the membrane potential to 2 bits leaves
accuracy at parity, $0.982$, yet the region its explanations highlight overlaps
the full-precision one by $0.44$, well below the numerical floor of $0.70$; a
4-bit weight model of the same accuracy keeps an overlap of $0.74$. The change
is real but bounded: the new explanations still point at evidence the model
uses, as the deletion metric confirms, just not at the evidence the original
model pointed at. The hypothesis stated above is not supported in the form
it was written --- both arms first move the explanation at the same bit-width,
and the 2-bit membrane changes \emph{where} the explanation points more than
\emph{when} --- but its mechanism is: the same quantization makes the spike
trains of the first layer perfectly periodic, a temporal change that the
temporal metric of the explanation does not register. The configuration with the
changed explanation is also dominated on energy and memory, so in this network
there is no trade-off to make: a joint 4-bit model keeps both accuracy and
explanation at about $1/60$ of the estimated energy and one eighth of the memory.

\section{Application domain}

The classification task is the identification of transient noise --- known as
\emph{glitches} --- in the data of the LIGO gravitational-wave detectors, using
the Gravity Spy release~\cite{zevin2017, glanzer2023, glanzer2021}.

The domain was chosen for three reasons. Explanation fidelity has an
\emph{operational} consequence there: glitch classification guides detector
characterization, where a commissioner acts physically on the instrument, so a
compressed model reaching the same label from different evidence would preserve
the label while corrupting the diagnostic cue. The classes have physically
interpretable morphology, so a domain expert can judge whether an attention map
falls where the glitch actually is, which is not true of a generic image
benchmark. And the data is public, large and labelled.

\section{Structure}

surrogate gradients, quantization for spiking architectures, explainability
