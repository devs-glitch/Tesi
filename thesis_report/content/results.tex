This chapter reports the outcome of the pipeline described in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} and~\ref{ch:hardware}. Section~\ref{sec:accuracy-results} quantifies the accuracy cost of severe quantization. Section~\ref{sec:xai-comparison} compares the software Temporal Spike Activation Maps of the baseline and quantized models. Section~\ref{sec:physical-validation} presents the core contribution of this thesis: the side-by-side comparison of theoretical software timesteps against physically measured oscilloscope latencies. Section~\ref{sec:tradeoffs} discusses the resulting memory/explainability trade-off and its implications for real astrophysical deployments.

\emph{The tables and figures in this chapter are laid out with the exact structure, columns, and captions that the final experimental campaign will populate; numerical placeholders are marked accordingly and are to be replaced with measured values once training, quantization, and HIL data collection (Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology}--\ref{ch:hardware}) have been executed to completion.}

Two outcomes are possible and are discussed explicitly once data is available. The quantized model may (i) \emph{preserve focus}, continuing to attribute its decision to the same physically meaningful time-frequency region as the baseline model, indicating that quantization compresses the representation without discarding the discriminative physical feature; or it may (ii) \emph{lose or shift focus}, attributing its decision to a different, potentially spurious region -- for instance, a quantization artefact or a region correlated with the class label only in the training distribution. The two cases are distinguished quantitatively using an overlap metric (e.g., Intersection-over-Union or normalized cross-correlation) between the baseline and quantized attribution maps $M_{t^{*}}$, aggregated per class, together with a comparison of the first-spike timestep $t^{*}$ itself: a large shift in $t^{*}$ between the baseline and the quantized model, even when the spatial map is preserved, indicates that quantization altered \emph{when} the network commits to a decision without altering \emph{what} it attends to, which is directly relevant to the physical validation of Section~\ref{sec:physical-validation}.

The theoretical expected physical delay is obtained by converting the software timestep $t^{*}$ into wall-clock time using the per-timestep execution time of the deployed inference loop (measured independently by profiling a fixed number of simulation steps on the microcontroller). Agreement between $\Delta t_{\text{phys}}$ and this theoretical expectation, within the timing resolution of the instrumentation and the jitter introduced by the microcontroller's own clock, would constitute physical validation of the software XAI method: it would demonstrate that the temporal explanation is not merely a simulation artefact, but a faithful description of the hardware's real-time decision-making process. Conversely, a systematic residual (e.g., a constant physical offset attributable to fixed per-layer execution overhead, or a residual that grows with $t^{*}$) would indicate that the software timestep is a directionally correct but not perfectly calibrated proxy for physical latency, a distinction that is discussed in relation to the limitations of Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The discussion frames this trade-off in terms of real deployment feasibility for astrophysical instrumentation: a quantization level is considered acceptable for a real Gravity Spy-adjacent deployment (e.g., an auxiliary real-time glitch triage system at a detector site) only if it preserves both classification accuracy above the threshold required for operational use and a physically faithful temporal explanation, since an explanation that cannot be trusted to reflect the hardware's actual behaviour undermines the very purpose of deploying an explainable model in a scientific pipeline. Where the two objectives conflict, this section identifies the Pareto-optimal bit-width for this specific microcontroller and dataset, and discusses whether a less aggressive quantization target, or a partially quantized network (e.g., full precision only in the earliest layers, where Section~\ref{sec:xai-comparison} is expected to show the discriminative feature is first extracted), offers a better compromise.
