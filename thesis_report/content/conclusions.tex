\section{Summary of Key Findings}
\label{sec:summary}
%------------------------------------------------------------

This thesis set out to answer a single question: does a severely quantized Spiking Neural Network still classify gravitational-wave detector glitches based on genuine physical features, and does the physical, hardware-measured timing of its response match what its software-generated temporal explanation predicted? Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} through~\ref{ch:results} pursued this question through the three objectives set out in Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

First, an SNN classifier was trained on a distinct subset of Gravity Spy glitch classes and compressed through Quantization-Aware Training applied jointly to synaptic weights and membrane potentials, down to 4-bit and 2-bit precision (Sections~\ref{sec:baseline-model}--\ref{sec:qat}), and the resulting accuracy retention -- or loss -- relative to the full-precision baseline was quantified in Section~\ref{sec:accuracy-results}. Second, Temporal Spike Activation Maps were extracted for both the baseline and quantized models (Section~\ref{sec:xai-extraction}) and compared in Section~\ref{sec:xai-comparison} to establish whether extreme quantization preserves the spatial and temporal locus of the network's decision. Third, and central to this thesis' contribution, the quantized model was ported to a microcontroller and its physical Time-to-First-Spike was measured through the Hardware-in-the-Loop, GPIO-toggling protocol of Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}, and directly compared against the software-predicted timestep $t^{*}$ in Section~\ref{sec:physical-validation}.

Once the experimental campaign described in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is completed, this section will state, explicitly and in relation to the objectives of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}: (i) the quantitative accuracy cost of each tested bit-width; (ii) whether the quantized model's software explanation remained aligned with the baseline's, or drifted toward different, potentially spurious features; and (iii) whether the physically measured hardware latency corroborated the theoretical software timestep within the resolution of the instrumentation, which is the physical validation -- or refutation -- of software-only temporal XAI claims for quantized SNNs that motivated this work (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).

\section{Limitations of the Study}
\label{sec:limitations}
%------------------------------------------------------------

This work is subject to several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its results, and that are acknowledged here with academic honesty rather than glossed over.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Von Neumann emulation, not a native neuromorphic substrate.} The microcontroller used in Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware} (ESP32/Teensy) is a conventional Von Neumann architecture that \emph{emulates} spiking, event-driven computation through a sequential instruction stream; it does not possess the asynchronous, event-driven circuitry of a true neuromorphic Application-Specific Integrated Circuit (ASIC) such as Intel's Loihi \cite{davies2018loihi}. The physical latencies measured in Section~\ref{sec:physical-validation} therefore reflect the timing of a software emulation of spiking dynamics on general-purpose silicon, not the timing of a genuinely event-driven neuromorphic processor, and the absolute energy and latency figures obtained here should not be extrapolated to native neuromorphic hardware without qualification.
    \item \textbf{I/O bottlenecks in feeding data to the board.} The HIL protocol of Section~\ref{sec:measurement-protocol} replays a precomputed, offline spike-encoded input to the microcontroller over a serial or SPI link. This transfer is itself a potential source of latency and jitter that is deliberately excluded from the measured $\Delta t_{\text{phys}}$ window (which starts only at \texttt{t0\_start}, after the input is already buffered on-device), but it means the reported timings do not capture the additional, and non-negligible, cost of streaming a live signal from a real gravitational-wave data-acquisition pipeline to the edge device in a genuinely online deployment.
    \item \textbf{Reduced dataset scope.} The experiments target a deliberately narrow, curated subset of Gravity Spy classes (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-prep}), chosen for morphological separability rather than full taxonomic coverage; the reported accuracy and explainability results should be understood as evidence for the specific classes tested, not as a claim of coverage across the entire Gravity Spy taxonomy.
    \item \textbf{Instrumentation resolution and clock jitter.} The physical validation of Section~\ref{sec:physical-validation} is bounded by the sampling resolution of the oscilloscope/logic analyzer and by clock jitter intrinsic to the microcontroller, both of which set a lower bound on the timing resolution at which software and physical predictions can be meaningfully distinguished.
    \item \textbf{Single-device generalization.} All hardware measurements are collected on one microcontroller model; timing behaviour, and therefore the degree of physical/software agreement, may differ on other embedded targets with different clock speeds, memory hierarchies, or compiler toolchains.
\end{itemize}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Future Directions}
\label{sec:future-work}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The findings and limitations above suggest several directions for follow-up work.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deployment on true neuromorphic hardware.} Repeating the exact HIL validation protocol of Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware} on a native, asynchronous neuromorphic ASIC (e.g., Intel Loihi \cite{davies2018loihi} or an equivalent research platform) would remove the Von Neumann emulation limitation identified in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} and test whether the software/physical timing agreement observed in this thesis holds, strengthens, or weakens on genuinely event-driven silicon.
    \item \textbf{XAI-driven dynamic early exit.} If Section~\ref{sec:physical-validation} confirms that the software-predicted first-spike timestep $t^{*}$ reliably anticipates physical decision latency, this signal could be exploited at inference time rather than only post-hoc: a dynamic \emph{early-exit} mechanism could terminate simulation once the network's running confidence indicates $t^{*}$ has likely already occurred, saving the energy and time otherwise spent simulating the remaining $T - t^{*}$ timesteps, directly turning this thesis' explainability method into a practical power-saving feature for edge deployments.
    \item \textbf{Broader dataset and class coverage.} Extending the pipeline to the full Gravity Spy taxonomy, and to auxiliary-channel data beyond spectrograms, would test whether the quantization/explainability trade-offs identified in Section~\ref{sec:tradeoffs} generalize beyond the curated subset used in this work.
    \item \textbf{Online, streaming HIL validation.} Addressing the I/O-bottleneck limitation by feeding the microcontroller a live or near-live simulated strain-data stream, rather than a precomputed offline buffer, would extend the physical validation of this thesis toward the conditions of an actual real-time detector-characterization deployment.
\end{itemize}
