\chapter{Software Methodology: SNN Design and Explainability}
\label{ch:methodology}
\setlength{\parskip}{1em}
%-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter describes the entirely software-side part of the pipeline: how the Gravity Spy data is prepared and spike-encoded (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-prep}), how the uncompressed baseline SNN is designed and trained (Section~\ref{sec:baseline-model}), how it is compressed through Quantization-Aware Training (Section~\ref{sec:qat}), and how Temporal Spike Activation Maps are extracted from both models (Section~\ref{sec:xai-extraction}). The output of this chapter -- a trained, quantized SNN and its associated temporal explanations -- is the input to the hardware pipeline described in Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Dataset Preparation and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:dataset-prep}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Rather than the full Gravity Spy taxonomy, this work selects a distinct, physically diverse subset of glitch classes, chosen to be visually and morphologically separable in the time-frequency domain (e.g., classes dominated by a compact high-energy transient versus classes dominated by a slowly modulating tone), so that the temporal location of the discriminative feature within the spectrogram is well defined and can later be compared against the network's temporal explanation.

For each selected class, the publicly available Gravity Spy Q-transform spectrograms are used as the starting representation. Each spectrogram is converted to grayscale, resized to a fixed resolution compatible with the input layer of the SNN, and normalized so that pixel intensity lies in $[0, 1]$. The dataset is split into training, validation, and test partitions, stratified by class to preserve the relative frequency of each glitch morphology.

Because SNNs operate on discrete-time spike trains rather than static images, each normalized spectrogram must be encoded into a sequence of $T$ binary frames before it can be presented to the network. This work uses \emph{rate coding} at the input layer: for a given pixel of normalized intensity $x \in [0,1]$, a spike is emitted at timestep $t$ with probability $x$, generated by comparing $x$ against $T$ independent samples of a Bernoulli process (implemented in \texttt{snnTorch} as a spike-generation utility). The resulting input is a $T \times H \times W$ binary tensor, where $H \times W$ is the spectrogram resolution, that is fed into the network one timestep at a time. The number of timesteps $T$ is chosen as a trade-off between reconstruction fidelity of the input encoding and the length of the temporal window that the TTFS-based explainability method (Section~\ref{sec:xai-extraction}) and the physical latency measurement (Section~\ref{sec:hil-architecture}) must resolve.

\section{Baseline Model Architecture and Training}
\label{sec:baseline-model}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The baseline model is a convolutional SNN, structurally analogous to a small CNN classifier but with every activation replaced by a Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron layer that is unrolled over $T$ timesteps. The topology consists of a stack of convolution -- LIF -- pooling blocks that progressively reduce the spatial resolution of the spectrogram while increasing the number of feature channels, followed by one or more fully connected LIF layers, and a final fully connected \emph{readout} layer of output LIF neurons, one per glitch class. All weights are initially trained at full (32-bit floating-point) precision, establishing the high-precision reference against which the quantized model of Section~\ref{sec:qat} is compared.

Each LIF neuron updates its membrane potential $U$ at timestep $t$ according to
\[
U[t] = \beta \, U[t-1] + I[t] - S[t-1]\, V_{th},
\]
where $I[t]$ is the weighted input current at timestep $t$, $\beta \in (0,1)$ is the membrane decay (leak) constant, $V_{th}$ is the firing threshold, and $S[t-1]$ is the binary spike emitted at the previous timestep (used here to implement a soft reset that subtracts the threshold rather than hard-resetting to zero, preserving residual information). A spike $S[t] = 1$ is emitted whenever $U[t] \geq V_{th}$.

Because the Heaviside spiking function has zero gradient almost everywhere, the network is trained with the \emph{surrogate gradient} method \cite{neftci2019surrogate}: during the forward pass the true (non-differentiable) spike function is used, while during the backward pass its derivative is replaced by a smooth surrogate (a fast-sigmoid approximation), allowing standard backpropagation through time (BPTT) to be applied across all $T$ timesteps with the \texttt{snnTorch} \cite{eshraghian2021training} and PyTorch autograd engines. The training objective combines a cross-entropy loss computed on the accumulated (summed or membrane-integrated) output spikes over the simulation window with the Adam optimizer, a fixed learning-rate schedule, and standard regularization (weight decay, early stopping on the validation split). Hyperparameters -- number of timesteps $T$, membrane decay $\beta$, firing threshold $V_{th}$, learning rate, and batch size -- are tuned on the validation split and reported alongside the resulting baseline accuracy in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) Strategy}
\label{sec:qat}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Once the full-precision baseline is trained, the same architecture is retrained (fine-tuned from the baseline weights) under \emph{Quantization-Aware Training} to simulate the severe memory limitations of a neuromorphic chip. Following the hardware-friendly quantization strategy for SNNs proposed in \cite{liu2026hardfsnn} and the general integer-arithmetic-only QAT formulation of \cite{jacob2018quantization}, quantization is applied to two distinct classes of parameters:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Synaptic weights.} Each convolutional and fully connected weight tensor is quantized to a low integer bit-width (the experiments in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} target 4-bit and 2-bit precision) using a per-layer, symmetric linear quantizer. During the forward pass, weights are rounded to the nearest representable quantization level; during the backward pass, the straight-through estimator (STE) is used to propagate gradients through the non-differentiable rounding operation, allowing the underlying full-precision ``shadow'' weights to keep learning to compensate for quantization error.
    \item \textbf{Membrane potentials.} Because membrane-potential storage can dominate an SNN's memory footprint \cite{liu2026hardfsnn}, the membrane potential $U[t]$ of every LIF neuron is likewise clipped to a fixed dynamic range and quantized to a low-bit-width fixed-point representation at every timestep, rather than only at the input/output boundaries of each layer. This is the more aggressive and more failure-prone of the two quantization targets, since accumulated rounding error in $U[t]$ compounds over the $T$ simulation steps and can directly shift the timestep at which a neuron reaches $V_{th}$ -- the exact quantity that this thesis' explainability method (Section~\ref{sec:xai-extraction}) and physical validation (Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware}) are built around.
\end{itemize}

The quantization-aware model is trained with the same loss, optimizer, and data pipeline as the baseline, differing only in the insertion of fake-quantization operators on weights and membrane potentials during the forward pass. The resulting integer-only weight tensors and quantization scale factors are what is ultimately exported for embedded deployment in Section~\ref{sec:porting}.

\section{Extraction of Temporal Spike Activation Maps}
\label{sec:xai-extraction}
%------------------------------------------------------------

To identify \emph{when} and \emph{where} each model bases its classification decision, this thesis extracts a \emph{Temporal Spike Activation Map} (TSAM) for both the baseline and the quantized model, for every correctly classified test sample. The procedure combines two ingredients at every timestep $t = 1, \dots, T$:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Per-timestep spatial attribution.} At each timestep $t$, the gradient of the winning class' output activation with respect to the feature maps of the last convolutional LIF layer is computed and global-average-pooled across channels to obtain per-channel importance weights, in direct analogy with Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}. These weights are used to produce a weighted combination of the feature maps at timestep $t$, yielding a spatial saliency map $M_t$ that highlights which time-frequency region of the input spectrogram is driving the network's activity at that specific simulation step.
    \item \textbf{First-spike time of the output neuron.} In parallel, the timestep $t^{*}$ at which the winning output neuron first crosses $V_{th}$ and emits its spike is recorded directly from the simulation, following the TTFS coding principle of Section~\ref{sec:snn-edge}. This is the theoretical, software-side prediction of when the network ``commits'' to its decision.
\end{enumerate}

Stacking $\{M_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ produces a spatio-temporal explanation volume, while $t^{*}$ identifies the single most decision-relevant slice of that volume. Overlaying $M_{t^{*}}$ on the original spectrogram shows \emph{where} in the time-frequency plane the network focuses at the moment it decides, and the sequence $M_1, \dots, M_{t^{*}}$ shows \emph{how} that focus evolves as evidence accumulates. This pair, $(M_{t^{*}}, t^{*})$, is computed for both the baseline and the quantized model and is the direct object of comparison in Section~\ref{sec:xai-comparison}, and $t^{*}$ specifically is the quantity that the Hardware-in-the-Loop protocol of Chapter~\ref{ch:hardware} attempts to physically reproduce and validate.
