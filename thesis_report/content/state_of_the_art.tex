\section{Spiking Neural Networks and Edge AI}
\label{sec:snn-edge}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Spiking Neural Networks are often described as the third generation of neural network models \cite{roy2019towards}. Unlike conventional Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), which propagate real-valued activations synchronously through dense multiply-and-accumulate operations, SNNs communicate through sparse, binary events (spikes) that are integrated over discrete timesteps by biologically inspired neuron models. The most widely used abstraction is the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron, whose membrane potential $V(t)$ accumulates weighted input spikes and leaks over time until it crosses a firing threshold $V_{th}$, at which point the neuron emits a spike and its potential is reset. Because information is encoded in the presence and timing of these events rather than in continuous activations, SNN inference can, in principle, be implemented with low-cost accumulate-only (AC) arithmetic, giving neuromorphic hardware platforms an intrinsic energy advantage over MAC-based accelerators \cite{roy2019towards, davies2018loihi}.

Training SNNs is complicated by the non-differentiability of the spike (Heaviside) function. This thesis follows the now-standard \emph{surrogate gradient} approach \cite{neftci2019surrogate}, in which the discontinuous derivative of the spiking non-linearity is replaced, only during the backward pass, with a smooth surrogate (e.g., a fast sigmoid or arctangent), allowing gradient-based optimization frameworks such as PyTorch to train SNNs end-to-end through Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT). The \texttt{snnTorch} library \cite{eshraghian2021training} implements this approach and is used throughout this work to define, train, and simulate the LIF-based network.

Among the possible information-coding schemes for SNNs (rate coding, temporal/latency coding, population coding), this thesis adopts \emph{Time-to-First-Spike} (TTFS) coding \cite{mostafa2017supervised}. In a TTFS scheme, information is encoded in the latency of the first spike emitted by a neuron after stimulus onset: a strongly excited neuron fires early, a weakly excited one fires late or not at all. TTFS coding is attractive for two reasons that are central to this thesis. First, it is inherently sparse -- at most one spike per neuron is needed to convey a decision, minimizing the number of switching events that must be measured on hardware. Second, and more importantly for this work, it provides a natural link between a \emph{software} quantity (the timestep $t$ at which a neuron first fires in simulation) and a \emph{physical} quantity (the wall-clock delay between stimulus presentation and the corresponding GPIO transition on a microcontroller), which is exactly the correlation this thesis sets out to validate.

Despite this theoretical efficiency, deploying SNNs on embedded and neuromorphic hardware is bottlenecked less by computation than by memory, a limitation commonly referred to as the \emph{memory wall}. Every LIF neuron must retain a persistent membrane potential across all $T$ simulation timesteps, in addition to the synaptic weight matrices already required by conventional networks; recent hardware-aware studies report that membrane-potential storage alone can dominate the total memory footprint of an SNN during inference \cite{liu2026hardfsnn}. \emph{Quantization-Aware Training} (QAT) \cite{jacob2018quantization} addresses this by simulating low-bit-width (e.g., 8-bit, 4-bit, or 2-bit) integer representations of weights and activations during training, so that the network learns to compensate for the discretization error before it is ever deployed. Applying QAT jointly to synaptic weights \emph{and} membrane potentials, as proposed for hardware-friendly SNNs \cite{liu2026hardfsnn}, is the compression strategy adopted in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} to fit the network within the memory budget of a commodity microcontroller.

\section{Temporal Explainability in SNNs}
\label{sec:temporal-xai}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Explainable AI (XAI) methods for conventional CNNs, most notably Class Activation Mapping (CAM) and its gradient-based generalization Grad-CAM \cite{selvaraju2017gradcam}, produce a single spatial heatmap that highlights the image regions most responsible for a classification decision. These techniques were designed for networks that perform one forward pass and therefore have no notion of \emph{when}, only \emph{where}, a decision was formed.

SNNs, in contrast, are dynamical systems that evolve over $T$ discrete timesteps, and the same input can be classified correctly at an early or a late timestep depending on how quickly informative evidence accumulates in the membrane potentials. Extending activation mapping to the temporal domain therefore requires tracking gradients or spike statistics not just across spatial (or spectro-temporal) input locations, but across simulation time as well. Recent work on visualizing spiking networks has shown that inter-spike statistics and gradient attributions computed per-timestep can be projected back onto the input to produce a stack of temporal saliency maps, one per timestep, rather than a single static heatmap \cite{roy2019towards, kim2021visual}. Building on this idea, this thesis implements a \emph{Temporal Spike Activation Map} (Section~\ref{sec:xai-extraction}) that combines (i) a Grad-CAM-style spatial attribution computed at each timestep $t$ with (ii) the network's own first-spike time, so that each attribution map is explicitly tagged with \emph{when}, during the $T$-step simulation, the network became confident enough to fire.

This temporal dimension is what makes physical validation possible. A purely spatial XAI method has nothing to validate against on an oscilloscope; a \emph{temporal} XAI method makes a falsifiable prediction (``the network should commit to its decision at simulated timestep $t^*$'') that can, in principle, be compared against a physically measured delay, which is precisely the contribution pursued in Chapters~\ref{ch:hardware} and~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Hardware-in-the-Loop Validation Profiling}
\label{sec:hil}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Software-only latency estimates (e.g., counting simulated timesteps, or profiling a Python inference loop) are confounded by simulation overhead, operating-system scheduling, and the numerical mismatch between floating-point simulation and integer hardware execution. To obtain a trustworthy physical latency measurement, this thesis follows the methodology established by \emph{Hardware-in-the-Loop} (HIL) benchmarking, in which the device under test executes the exact deployed model while an external, independent instrument measures the physical timing of its output.

The reference benchmark for this practice in the TinyML community is MLPerf Tiny \cite{banbury2021mlperftiny}, which standardizes latency and energy measurement on microcontroller-class devices by requiring the device to toggle a dedicated GPIO pin to signal the start and end of inference, while an external timer, oscilloscope, or logic analyzer captures the interval. This removes any dependency on the microcontroller's own (potentially inaccurate or interrupt-jittered) internal clock and any communication latency of a serial link, isolating \emph{pure} inference time. This thesis adopts the same principle at a finer grain: instead of toggling a GPIO pin only at the start and end of a full inference pass, a pin is toggled at the exact moment the output neuron representing the winning class emits its first spike, allowing the oscilloscope trace itself to serve as a physical measurement of TTFS latency (Section~\ref{sec:hil-architecture}).

\section{The Gravity Spy Dataset}
\label{sec:gravityspy}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The Advanced LIGO and Advanced Virgo interferometers detect gravitational waves by measuring minute changes in the length of kilometer-scale laser arms \cite{abbott2016observation}. These instruments are exquisitely sensitive, and consequently also exquisitely susceptible to non-astrophysical noise transients, or \emph{glitches}, caused by environmental disturbances (e.g., seismic activity, anthropogenic noise) and instrumental artefacts (e.g., scattered light, electronics crosstalk). Glitches can mimic or mask genuine gravitational-wave signals, and reliably identifying and categorizing them is essential to protect the scientific integrity of detection claims.

Gravity Spy \cite{zevin2017gravityspy} is a citizen-science project that combines volunteer classification with machine learning to characterize LIGO/Virgo glitches. Each glitch is represented as a spectrogram -- a time-frequency image produced by a Q-transform of the strain data -- and assigned to one of several dozen morphological classes (e.g., \emph{Blip}, \emph{Koi Fish}, \emph{Whistle}, \emph{Scattered Light}), each associated with a distinct, physically motivated noise source. Because these classes are visually and physically distinguishable in the time-frequency domain, Gravity Spy spectrograms are a natural benchmark for both standard CNN classifiers and, as explored in this thesis, SNNs that convert spectrogram intensity into spike trains (Section~\ref{sec:dataset-prep}). Reliable, explainable classification of these glitches directly supports detector characterization efforts and reduces the risk that instrumental artefacts are misidentified as astrophysical events.
